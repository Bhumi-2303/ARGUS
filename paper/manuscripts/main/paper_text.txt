Cross-Domain Generalization Failure in IoT/SCADA Intrusion Detection: Representation Resolution and Class-Aware AdaptationAuthor name withheld for review — ARGUS ProjectIndependent Researcher[affiliation and contact details to be added before submission]AbstractIntrusion detection systems (IDS) for IoT and industrial control (SCADA) networks are typically evaluated within a single collection domain. We study what happens when a fixed, low-dimensional feature representation is forced across three heterogeneous domains: CICIoT2023 (D1), NF-ToN-IoT-v2 (D2), and IEC 60870-5-104 SCADA traffic (D3). Between D1 and D2, XGBoost and LightGBM reach 94.9–98.6% in-domain F1 but collapse to near-chance ROC-AUC (0.32–0.69) cross-domain; Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistics (0.37–0.74) show the shift is class-conditional, and a leakage-controlled class-aware CORAL adaptation recovers substantial balanced accuracy (0.497→0.711) and MCC (−0.031→0.386), while a domain-adversarial (DANN) alternative — now fully resolved against the project&apos;s own raw exports — converges to a majority-class classifier (ROC-AUC = 0.332). Extending the same common representation toward D3 fails even more severely (ROC-AUC 0.44–0.61), and, contrary to a smooth-improvement claim circulated internally, we find representation size and transfer performance are related non-monotonically: an 8-feature variant collapses to near-chance (ROC-AUC = 0.445, MCC = 0.000), worse than both the 4- and 6-feature variants. Only abandoning cross-domain comparability in favor of D3&apos;s full 70-feature native representation restores non-trivial discrimination (ROC-AUC 0.65–0.67), and even then attack recall stays below 10% under realistic SOC false-positive budgets. We report these findings — including corrections to several previously circulated but unverified figures — as evidence that cross-domain IDS failure is substantially a representation problem rather than a pure model-capacity problem, while explicitly flagging the representation-collapse statistics and protocol-aware SCADA features proposed elsewhere as still unverified.Keywords: cross-domain generalization, IoT/SCADA intrusion detection, domain shift, representation resolution, domain adaptation, CORAL, DANN, FT-Transformer, explainability1. INTRODUCTIONIntrusion detection models for IoT and industrial control (SCADA) networks are usually built and evaluated with an implicit assumption: that the network the model will eventually monitor looks statistically similar to the network it was trained on. Benchmark datasets such as CICIoT2023 [1] and NetFlow-based ToN-IoT variants [2] have made it easy to reach this assumption without testing it, because same-dataset evaluation protocols cannot distinguish a model that has learned transferable attack behavior from one that has learned dataset-specific traffic idiosyncrasies.Real smart-grid and industrial environments differ from generic IoT capture environments in traffic mix, protocol semantics, and attack taxonomy, so this assumption is exactly where cross-domain deployment is most likely to fail. When two domains are forced into a common feature representation so that a classifier trained on one can be evaluated on the other, the representation itself becomes a design variable: an overly compact representation may discard the information a target domain needs to separate benign from attack traffic, independent of which classifier consumes it. Existing domain-adaptation methods such as CORAL [6] and DANN [7] were developed to correct distributional mismatch given a fixed representation; they have no mechanism to recover information the representation never captured in the first place.This paper investigates that failure systematically across three domains — CICIoT2023 (D1), NF-ToN-IoT-v2 (D2), and IEC 60870-5-104 SCADA traffic (D3) — and asks six research questions: (RQ1) how severe is cross-domain degradation under a common representation; (RQ2) is it caused by model failure or by information loss in the shared representation; (RQ3) can class-aware adaptation recover performance when the representation retains enough information; (RQ4) how does representation resolution (feature count) affect cross-domain and native-domain performance; (RQ5) does cross-domain performance remain useful under realistic security-operations-center (SOC) false-positive constraints; and (RQ6) can richer, domain-native representations recover information that a generic flow-level representation cannot capture. We answer RQ6 only partially: we can show that D3&apos;s full native representation outperforms the compact cross-domain representation, but we cannot yet show that a protocol-aware (application-layer-semantic) SCADA representation does the same, because no leakage-free experiment of that kind exists in this project at the time of writing.This paper makes four contributions, each stated at the strength the evidence supports: (1) a controlled, bidirectional D1↔D2 cross-domain evaluation showing severe, class-conditional degradation; (2) a fully resolved comparison of source-only, global CORAL, class-aware CORAL, and DANN adaptation under a leakage-controlled protocol, correcting a previously unresolved DANN metrics conflict; (3) an extension of the same representation toward a third domain (D3, SCADA) showing that transfer failure is not specific to D1↔D2 or to tree-based models, together with a verified representation-resolution comparison that corrects a smooth-improvement claim we found was not supported by the underlying data; and (4) an explicit, itemized account of which claims in this line of work remain unverified, rather than a paper that presents its strongest possible narrative.2. PROBLEM FORMULATIONLet a domain be a distribution over (X, Y) pairs, where X is a feature vector and Y ∈ {0, 1} is a benign/attack label. Two domains D_A and D_B can differ in class priors P(Y), in the feature distribution P(X), and in the class-conditional feature distribution P(X | Y); any of the three can independently break a classifier trained on D_A and evaluated on D_B, and they are not mutually exclusive [5].A common feature representation is a mapping φ from each domain&apos;s native feature space into a shared space, chosen so a single classifier&apos;s input schema is valid for both domains. We define the representation&apos;s cardinality as the number of distinct feature vectors φ produces over a domain&apos;s traffic, and representation collapse as the situation where φ maps a large number of distinguishable native-feature examples onto a small number of shared-space points, destroying information the target domain would need to separate its own classes — independent of any cross-domain distributional shift. A sufficiently aggressive φ can therefore fail a target domain even if source and target shared-space distributions were perfectly aligned, because alignment cannot restore information φ already discarded.This framing motivates the representation-resolution question directly: if cross-domain failure is largely a P(X)/P(X|Y) shift problem within a fixed, information-preserving φ, then domain adaptation (CORAL, DANN) should recover most of the loss; if it is substantially a representation-collapse problem, adaptation methods operating on the collapsed space should show limited benefit, and only increasing what φ preserves should help. Section V tests both predictions against verified evidence.3. METHODOLOGYFig. 1 summarizes the evaluation framework used throughout this paper.Fig. 1. ARGUS evaluation framework: D1↔D2 cross-domain diagnosis and adaptation, extended with D3 native and transfer evaluation.Common feature harmonization. The primary cross-domain representation, ARGUS-4, consists of four behavioral features derivable from both D1 and D2&apos;s native schemas: pkt_mean_to_max, tcp_flag_density, log_pkt_mean, and log_pkt_max. To test whether representation size drives cross-domain performance, two further common representations, ARGUS-6 and ARGUS-8, extend ARGUS-4 with additional shared behavioral fields; we treat ARGUS-4 as the primary, most-tested representation and ARGUS-6/ARGUS-8 as a resolution-scaling probe (Section V-D).Source-only baselines. XGBoost [8] and LightGBM [9] are trained on D1 or D2 with no adaptation and evaluated on the other domain, establishing the degradation a mitigation method must address.CORAL and class-aware CORAL. Global CORAL [6] aligns the full joint second-order feature statistics between source and target. Class-aware CORAL aligns statistics separately per class; one variant estimates target class statistics using D2 test labels and is a diagnostic only — it is reported solely to explain the failure mechanism and is excluded from every leakage-controlled comparison in this paper. A second, “clean” class-aware CORAL variant estimates target class statistics on an adaptation split that never touches test labels.DANN. The domain-adversarial neural network [7] learns a representation from which a discriminator cannot recover the domain label, using unlabeled target adaptation data. Its D1→D2 result was, at the start of this project, reported inconsistently across two metric exports (ROC-AUC 0.332 vs. 0.523). We treat this as resolved in this version: the authoritative recomputation against the DANN model&apos;s raw prediction export gives ROC-AUC = 0.332, MCC = 0.013, and we no longer report the 0.523 figure. We note for transparency that this reconciliation was reported to us rather than independently re-derived from a raw prediction file inside the present analysis session — it is consistent with, and corroborated by, three independently exported aggregate CSVs we did check ourselves (Section V-C) — so we treat it as resolved rather than unverified, while distinguishing that level of confidence from findings we recomputed from raw per-sample predictions ourselves (e.g., Section V-E).FT-Transformer. FT-Transformer [10] is a Transformer-based architecture for tabular data, included to test whether cross-domain and representation-resolution failures are specific to tree-based classifiers. We use it both as a D1-representation cross-domain transfer model (ARGUS-4/6/8 → D3) and as the primary model family for the D3 native in-domain benchmark, at three capacity tiers (SMALL/MEDIUM/LARGE).Native SCADA representation. Independent of the cross-domain representations, D3 has its own native 70-field flow representation. Models trained and evaluated entirely within D3 on this representation establish an in-domain ceiling, not a cross-domain transfer result; the two must not be conflated (Section V-B).Feature-resolution scaling. We compare ARGUS-4, ARGUS-6, ARGUS-8, and D3&apos;s native 70-feature representation to test whether cross-domain (or native) discriminability scales smoothly with representation size, or whether it degrades non-monotonically at some intermediate sizes (Section V-D).SHAP analysis. We use SHAP [11] to compare which features drive predictions in each training domain, for both the D1↔D2 comparison and, where evidence exists, before/after representation changes. SHAP attribution describes association with model output, not a causal mechanism.Operational, FPR-constrained evaluation. Aggregate F1 can look acceptable even when a classifier is nearly useless at the false-positive rates a security operations center can tolerate. We additionally report recall at fixed FPR budgets (0.1%, 1%, 5%) wherever a raw per-sample prediction file makes this computable.Frozen-test protocol. For every leakage-controlled result in this paper, the target domain is split into three disjoint roles: an adaptation split (used only for unlabeled CORAL/DANN fitting), a calibration split (used only for threshold selection), and a frozen test split, evaluated exactly once per seeded model after the pipeline is fully fixed. Target test labels are never used for adaptation or threshold selection in any result we report as valid; the one CORAL variant that violates this rule is explicitly labeled diagnostic-only throughout and excluded from every comparison table.4. DISCUSSIONThe verified evidence supports a narrower and more specific claim than “ARGUS solves cross-domain intrusion detection”: within this project, cross-domain failure has a representation-mismatch component that is distinct from, and not fixed by, either global statistical alignment or a domain-adversarial objective. D1↔D2 fails severely and asymmetrically (Section V-A); global CORAL&apos;s failure despite near-perfect marginal alignment (Section V-C) is exactly what a class-conditional-shift explanation predicts; and extending the same style of compact representation toward D3 fails even more severely, in a way that does not improve smoothly with added features (Section V-D) — an 8-feature representation is worse than a 4-feature one, which is difficult to explain as anything other than the added features encoding domain-identifying rather than attack-discriminating information for that particular combination.Class-aware adaptation is the one mechanism in this paper that reliably helps, and only when class-conditional structure survives harmonization: clean class-aware CORAL recovers substantial balanced accuracy and MCC on D1→D2 (Section V-C), while DANN, now resolved, shows the alternative failure mode — collapsing to a degenerate, near-constant prediction rather than learning a genuinely domain-invariant representation. This is consistent with, though does not by itself prove, the idea that adversarial objectives can find a trivial equilibrium (fool the domain discriminator by discarding class-relevant information too) when the underlying task is already this hard.F1 is misleading throughout this study in a specific, recurring way: DANN&apos;s F1 (0.841) and the D3 native ceiling&apos;s F1 (≈ 0.43) both look more informative in isolation than they are once paired with ROC-AUC, MCC, or FPR-constrained recall. Under severe class imbalance, a classifier that is heavily biased toward the majority class can post a respectable F1 while being close to useless for an analyst deciding which alerts to act on — which is why Section V-E&apos;s FPR-constrained recall (below 10% even at a 5% FPR budget for the best D3 model available) is a more operationally honest number than any aggregate metric in this paper.We are not able to say, on current evidence, whether SCADA intrusion detection specifically needs protocol-aware, application-layer-semantic features (as opposed to richer flow-level features of the kind D3&apos;s native 70-feature representation already provides). The native representation&apos;s own ceiling (ROC-AUC 0.646–0.674) is a meaningful improvement over cross-domain transfer, but is still far from a deployable detector, so representation resolution is necessary but plausibly not sufficient.7. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKFour findings summarize this study, stated at the confidence level the evidence supports. First, D1↔D2 cross-domain intrusion detection fails severely and asymmetrically under a compact common representation, despite strong in-domain performance for both classifiers tested (verified). Second, class-aware domain adaptation recovers substantial balanced accuracy and MCC when class-conditional structure survives harmonization, while global alignment and DANN do not (verified, DANN conflict now resolved). Third, representation resolution is not smoothly related to cross-domain or native performance in this project&apos;s own data: an 8-feature representation is worse than both a 4- and a 6-feature one, and only D3&apos;s full native representation clearly helps — which directly contradicts a smoother-improvement claim we found circulating without evidentiary support (verified against the project&apos;s own export). Fourth, aggregate metrics such as F1 and ROC-AUC materially overstate operational usefulness once evaluated under realistic SOC false-positive budgets, where the best available D3 model retains under 10% attack recall (verified).We explicitly do not conclude that representation collapse, in the specific entropy/tuple-count sense proposed elsewhere in this project, explains these results — that specific mechanism remains unverified. Future work should, in priority order: (1) produce and check the actual tuple-cardinality/entropy analysis before using it as evidence; (2) resolve REP-01&apos;s status and either report a corrected, leakage-audited result or drop it from the project&apos;s claims entirely; (3) obtain raw per-sample predictions for the D1↔D2 baselines so Table III and Table IV can be verified to the same standard as the D3 results in this paper; (4) run multi-seed D1↔D2 experiments with DeLong, McNemar, and bootstrap-CI statistical testing; and (5) determine, with an actual experiment rather than a claim, whether protocol-aware SCADA features add anything beyond D3&apos;s existing native 70-feature ceiling.REFERENCES[1] E.
